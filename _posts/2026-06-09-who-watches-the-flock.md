Picture every car you drove past today.

Now picture a camera reading the plate on each one, noting the make and color, stamping the time and place, and dropping all of it into a database that a police department three states away can search.

You didn’t agree to any of that. You weren’t suspected of anything. You just went to work.

That’s not a thought experiment. It is a system Macon-Bibb County already pays for. And on June 16, the commission votes on the FY2027 budget that lists continuing Flock as a public-safety goal.

I write about digital privacy and local government, so a company like Flock Safety sits right at the intersection of two things I can’t stop poking at. I’ve spent the last couple of weeks reading everything I can find about it. Some of what I found is genuinely useful technology. A lot of it scared me.

This post is my attempt to lay out why, and what you can do about it before the vote.

## A budget hearing, and a deadline

On June 2, the county held a public hearing on its FY2027 budget. More than half of that budget goes to public safety.

One after another, residents spoke against the county’s contract with Flock, the company behind the license-plate cameras you’ve seen sprouting on poles around town. Several people pointed out that the cameras appear to cluster in poorer parts of the city. One resident, Atlas August, said the cameras were *“working against that”* better future he wants for Macon. [WGXA was there.](https://wgxa.tv/news/local/citizens-and-leaders-trade-talks-on-flock-cameras-and-shotspotters-at-commission-meeting)

Sheriff David Davis defended the program. He said the cameras helped identify suspects in roughly 100 cases this year, from murder to armed robbery, and said the program should never be seen as targeting anyone.

The county’s budget draft lists keeping Flock as a goal for the year ahead. The commission also renewed its ShotSpotter gunshot-detection contract for three more years, at more than half a million dollars annually. Mayor Lester Miller said the budget gets its final vote on Tuesday, June 16.

So this is real. It is local. And the clock is running.

A little backstory: the Sheriff’s Office [started using Flock in 2023](https://wgxa.tv/news/project-macon/project-macon-are-surveillance-cameras-watching-you-or-criminals) and later landed a [$1.6 million state grant](https://wgxa.tv/news/local/flock-cameras) for Flock cameras.

There is also a public-private piece called “Operation Safer Together,” which [asks local businesses to connect their own camera systems with the Sheriff’s Office](https://wgxa.tv/news/local/could-safer-together-flock-cameras-help-curb-crime-in-macon-bibb-businesses). The Sheriff’s Office says the program is not meant for constant livestream monitoring and that access is limited. But the practical effect is still a larger shared camera system.

And two years ago, the ACLU of Georgia’s Christopher Bruce [told a local reporter](https://wgxa.tv/news/project-macon/project-macon-are-surveillance-cameras-watching-you-or-criminals) the thing I keep coming back to: a Macon plate scan doesn’t stay in Macon. It goes into *“a registry that’s used federally.”*

To see why that matters, you have to understand what the cameras actually do.

## What a Flock camera does

A Flock camera takes a picture of the back of your car. Software reads the plate and picks out the make, model, color, and identifying details, down to a bumper sticker. The company calls that a “vehicle fingerprint.”

It tags the time and location, sends the data to the cloud, and checks it against crime databases. If there is a hit, it pings an officer. Flock often describes ALPR data as being kept for short periods, commonly around 30 days, though retention can vary depending on law, contract terms, and approved extensions.

If it stopped there, this would be a much shorter post.

The part that changes everything is the network.

Your local police do not just search the cameras they own. They can search a statewide and, in practice, nationwide pool stitched together from billions of scans a month across thousands of communities.

That pool is the whole business. Reuters reported in March 2025 that Flock had raised $275 million and reached a valuation of $7.5 billion. The reason is the network effect. Every new camera and every new agency makes the whole web more valuable.

Think of it like a phone book nobody asked to be in.

You never signed up. You did nothing wrong. But every time you pass a camera on the way to work, a clinic, a protest, or a friend’s house, you get another line in the book. And the book is searchable by people you will never meet, in places you have never been.

One more thing, because it is a little personal: Flock is a Georgia company, founded in Atlanta in 2017 and now building a factory in the state. I would love to feel some hometown pride about that.

Then I read the reporting.

## The part that worries me

Let me be careful here, because the difference between “documented” and “alleged” matters. Some of what follows is settled fact: court rulings, public records, and a letter from a sitting U.S. senator. Some of it is reported allegation that is still being fought over. I will say which is which.

But the pattern is hard to ignore.

The civil-liberties groups watching Flock, including the EFF, the ACLU, and the Institute for Justice, keep making the same argument. After reading a year of this, I think they are right: the problems are not just a few rogue cops. They are baked into how the system is built and how the company runs it.

### A door for ICE that was never supposed to be there

In May 2025, [404 Media](https://www.404media.co/ice-taps-into-nationwide-ai-enabled-camera-network-data-shows/) obtained police records from Danville, Illinois, showing that local and state agencies had run more than 4,000 immigration-related searches of the Flock network in about a year.

The reasons typed into the search box included “immigration,” “ICE,” and “ICE WARRANT.” The reporters described it as a side door into a tool that federal immigration enforcement had no formal contract to use.

Later reporting found that [Customs and Border Protection had reached more than 80,000 cameras](https://www.404media.co/cbp-had-access-to-more-than-80-000-flock-ai-cameras-nationwide/), and that [ICE, the Secret Service, and even the Navy](https://www.404media.co/ice-secret-service-navy-all-had-access-to-flocks-nationwide-network-of-cameras/) had access through one arrangement or another.

Flock says it does not work with ICE and says it has ended federal pilot programs. I believe both can be true.

The searches still happened. They happened through favors and pilots, on the system Flock built and sold.

### A deputy in Texas, an abortion, and 83,000 cameras

This is the one I can’t shake.

[404 Media](https://www.404media.co/a-texas-cop-searched-license-plate-cameras-nationwide-for-a-woman-who-got-an-abortion/) reported, and the [EFF later confirmed with court documents](https://www.eff.org/deeplinks/2025/10/flock-safety-and-texas-sheriff-claimed-license-plate-search-was-missing-person-it), that a sheriff’s deputy in Johnson County, Texas, ran a nationwide search across roughly 83,000 cameras looking for one specific woman.

The reason he typed was *“had an abortion, search for female.”*

The office first called it a welfare check. The affidavit the EFF obtained showed it was a death investigation tied to a non-viable fetus. The search pulled in data from Washington and Illinois, two states that have strong protections for abortion access.

I wrote a long piece last year about the [STOP CSAM Act](https://www.kerryhatcher.com/digital-privacy-and-freedoms-are-under-threat/) and the slippery slope of “build it for the good guys.”

Here’s the slope, with a real person at the bottom of it.

Build the capability, and it gets aimed wherever someone with a login decides to aim it.

### Protesters. Including ours.

In November 2025, the EFF [worked through more than 12 million Flock searches](https://www.eff.org/deeplinks/2025/11/how-cops-are-using-flock-safetys-alpr-network-surveil-protesters-and-activists) logged by about 3,900 agencies.

They found more than 50 agencies, federal, state, and local, running searches tied to protests: the “No Kings” demonstrations, “Hands Off,” and the 50501 movement. Nineteen agencies ran searches specifically tied to No Kings.

I stood in one of those crowds.

Last summer, I [wrote about the No Kings protest in downtown Macon](https://www.kerryhatcher.com/no-kings-protest/). Hundreds of peaceful people were there. My neighbors were there.

The idea that showing up to something like that could land your plate in a database query is not theoretical to me anymore.

### Slurs in the search box

That same EFF analysis found something else that was just ugly.

More than 80 agencies used anti-Romani slurs and stereotypes as their stated search reasons, often without naming any crime at all.

Remember, the “reason” field is supposed to be the accountability mechanism. It is the thing officials point to when they say the system is safe.

It turns out to be a free-text box that depends entirely on whoever is typing into it being honest and decent.

### A people-search tool, built partly on breach data

[404 Media](https://www.404media.co/license-plate-reader-company-flock-is-building-a-massive-people-lookup-tool-leak-shows/) obtained leaked internal materials for a Flock product called Nova.

The product was designed to connect plate data to people using people-search tools, data brokers, and data from breaches.

After employees inside the company objected and the story ran, Flock said Nova would not use breach data. An EFF analyst called the original idea a dystopian panopticon. Flock said the report was premature.

### They can’t even keep the cameras locked

In December 2025, [404 Media and the researcher Benn Jordan](https://www.404media.co/flock-exposed-its-ai-powered-cameras-to-the-internet-we-tracked-ourselves/) showed that Flock had left livestreams and admin panels for at least 60 of its Condor PTZ cameras open on the public internet.

No password.

Anyone could watch live, download a month of archived video, and change the settings. Jordan verified it by watching himself walk around.

Then there is the login problem.

On November 3, 2025, [Senator Ron Wyden and Representative Raja Krishnamoorthi asked the FTC to investigate Flock](https://www.wyden.senate.gov/news/press-releases/wyden-krishnamoorthi-urge-ftc-to-investigate-surveillance-tech-company-on-negligently-handling-americans-personal-data). Their letter says passwords for at least 35 Flock customer accounts had already been stolen, and argues that the company’s failure to require two-factor login is likely an unfair business practice.

Flock’s answer was that two-factor login is on by default for new customers and that 97% of its police customers have turned it on.

Which is another way of saying some of them hadn’t.

### Arrested over a typo

This is where it stops being abstract.

A “7” misread as a “2” got Brandon Upchurch pulled over in Toledo, bitten by a police dog, and jailed. He won a $35,000 settlement.

The Herron family in Tennessee was stopped at gunpoint, with a three-year-old in the car, after a camera turned “LOVEY” into “L0VEY.”

A *Business Insider* and CBS review counted around a dozen wrongful-stop cases tied to license-plate-reader mistakes.

Flock’s response is that misreads help train better models.

That is cold comfort if it is your kid in the back seat.

### How Flock acts when someone pushes back

This is the part that tips me from “uneasy about license-plate cameras in general” to “uneasy about this company in particular.”

When a volunteer started mapping camera locations on a site called DeFlock, Flock sent a trademark cease-and-desist. The EFF took the case. The project did not fold.

Langley also emailed a Virginia police chief out of the blue, framing the company’s critics as a coordinated attack by people who want to defund the police. The chief was not having it, and [Staunton canceled its contract](https://san.com/cc/democracy-in-action-cities-react-to-privacy-concerns-by-canceling-flock-surveillance-contracts/).

Texas regulators hit Flock with a cease-and-desist for operating as an unlicensed security business from 2019 to 2024. Cambridge, Massachusetts, tore up its contract after the company installed cameras the city did not even know about. Flock has admitted it gave inaccurate information about federal pilot programs.

Add it up, and somewhere between 30 and 50-plus places have canceled, paused, or rejected Flock since early 2025. An Austin audit found that more than 20% of searches there were not properly documented. Denver moved to a competitor. Mountain View pulled the plug after learning federal agencies had been in its data.

The courts have not settled this. A federal judge in Norfolk, Virginia, ruled in January that the city’s use was constitutional on the record before him, while noting that Fourth Amendment analysis has to remain nimble as surveillance technology changes.

The plaintiffs, backed by the Institute for Justice, are appealing. The EFF and ACLU are challenging searches in San Jose, and a wave of class actions is rolling through California.

“Legal for now” is not the same as “fine.”

## The bigger worry, briefly

Set the company’s specific stumbles aside for a second, because even a flawless version of this raises a hard question.

A nationwide license-plate network builds a searchable record of where ordinary, innocent people go, with no suspicion that anyone did anything wrong.

The Supreme Court wrestled with this exact problem in *Carpenter v. United States*. The concern there was not any single location ping. It was the aggregation. A thousand harmless dots can become a portrait of your life.

Catching a stolen car with a few cameras is one thing. A persistent map of everyone’s movements, kept and shared by default, is something else entirely, even if every operator is a saint.

And these systems have a gravity all their own.

Once the infrastructure exists, the list of uses only grows. It started with stolen cars and missing kids. The documented record now includes immigration, abortion, and protests.

That is not a prediction. That is the reporting.

## In fairness to Flock

I do not want to flatten the other side, so here is the company’s case as straight as I can put it.

Flock says it does not work with ICE and says it has ended federal pilot programs. It says customers own and control their own data. It says federal sharing is now off by default.

The company also says it has added safeguards meant to restrict sensitive searches, improve auditing, and limit sharing. It says two-factor login is on by default for new customers and that most law-enforcement customers have enabled it.

Flock says the technology helped with more than a million investigations last year and around 10,000 missing-person recoveries. It also argues that many cancellations are driven by politics, not real misuse.

Some of those are real safeguards. The public-safety wins are real too, and I do not want to pretend otherwise.

But the concern remains: a human can work around the safeguard at nearly every level.

A filter you can route around. A reason box you can lie in. A default you can flip.

Those are policies, not laws of physics.

And we have now watched, again and again, what happens when the policy meets a motivated person with a password.

## So what about June 16?

I am not against solving crime, and I am not going to pretend Macon does not have real problems to solve.

But “it helped in 100 cases” and “it builds a permanent, shareable record of everyone’s movements” are not mutually exclusive. They can both be true.

The second one is the price of the first.

The county has already [spent something like $4.5 million on camera systems](https://wgxa.tv/news/local/could-safer-together-flock-cameras-help-curb-crime-in-macon-bibb-businesses) and points to falling homicides, while admitting it is not clear how much these programs specifically contributed.

The residents who noted that the cameras sit mostly in poorer neighborhoods are naming a fairness question that deserves a real answer, not a pat on the head.

If our commissioners are going to keep buying this, the bar ought to be high.

And here is the small hopeful part: every abuse I described above surfaced through reason fields and audit logs. That means oversight is possible.

Somebody just has to insist on it.

## What you can do before the vote

- **See what’s watching you.** Look up nearby cameras at [DeFlock.me](https://deflock.me/).
- **Ask for the records.** File an open-records request for the Sheriff’s Office Flock audit logs and the search “reason” fields. That is exactly how Austin found its undocumented-search problem.
- **Show up, or write in.** The [FY2027 budget is posted here](https://www.maconbibb.us/proposedfy27budget/). The vote is June 16. Tell your commissioner what you think.
- **Ask for specifics, not reassurance.** Ask for a documented case number and reason for every search. Ask for independent review of the audit logs. Ask for federal and nationwide sharing to be off by default. Ask for two-factor login to be required for everyone. Ask for the shortest retention the law allows. Ask for contract terms that bar immigration and abortion-related searches, with penalties that bite.
- **Pass this along** to someone who drives through Macon every day. That is basically everyone.

I would rather raise this now and be argued with than stay quiet and wonder later.

The data is public. The vote is public. The least we can do is ask the hard questions while we still have the room to.
